# I've Designed Systems Like This. Now I'm the Customer

I spend my career designing processes and systems that affect how people work and live. So when I became a *user* of a system that felt familiar in all the wrong ways, I recognized the pattern immediately. But recognizing it and *experiencing* it are two very different things.

## Last Year

My wife Wendy and I arrived at Trails End Campground on a Tuesday in May. The place had just opened for the season. There were roughly 50 unoccupied sites. We drove around, fell in love with how our RV fit just right at a particular spot--good sun exposure, close to water, room to set up for the summer. We moved in. It was perfect.

That simplicity mattered more than I realized at the time.

## This Year

We showed up the same week, same plan: wait for the weekend crowd, then spend Tuesday doing what we'd always done. Pick from dozens of options. Fall in love with one. Settle in.

Except this time, the system had changed. There were still 50-ish unoccupied sites. But only about 5 were available for a 90-day stay. Why? Because each reservation--even a single night--locked that site from everyone else. A site booked for one day in June was unavailable for our entire stay.

The paradox hit hard: the longer our commitment, the fewer our options. Bigger commitment = smaller choice.

We could see 45 sites we would have loved. We had access to 5. None were quite right.

## What I Recognized

I've made decisions like this before. And I almost missed what was happening.

The system moved from two sources of truth to one:

**Last year:** The terrain was the source of truth. Real sites. Real people on-site. Real time. If a site was empty, it was available. Simple.

**This year:** A database model became the source of truth. Sites locked by people we'd never met, in times we couldn't predict, for stays that might not happen. The map replaced the territory.

Two dimensions of lock-in:

- **Space lock-in:** Sites picked online, sight-unseen, without the feeling of whether they'd actually work
- **Time lock-in:** Guaranteed availability for future arrivals, but flexibility removed for present people

## Three Quick Examples

A guest named Jan needed a dog-friendly site. She'd booked online, arrived to find an electric fence next to her assigned site. Couldn't know that online. Needed staff to fix it.

Steven preferred shade. His pre-assigned site was sunnier than expected. Would have picked differently if he'd walked the grounds first.

Kevin wanted a site with the right tree for his hammock. Couldn't determine that from a map.

All three required staff intervention to resolve--or went unresolved.

## The Real Problem

None of these problems needed to exist.

The system could have guaranteed "you'll have a site when you arrive" without pre-assigning which specific site. It could have preserved the joy of walking around, falling in love, and choosing--the part that made the experience work last year.

Instead, it coupled two separate decisions: "Is there room?" and "Which site is yours?" They moved together. One to the database, one to the map, both locked in time.

It's easy to make this mistake. Full-featured systems make it *especially* easy--they have the features to do both things, so both get done. By the same system. At the same time. Without anyone asking: "Do these need to move together?"

## Why This Matters

I recognize this because I've inflicted it. In my career, I've made versions of this call. Good intentions. Solving one problem (guarantee availability). Creating another (removing autonomy). Not realizing until someone *became a customer* and told me.

The territory is not the map. Online models are useful for some things. But they're a poor substitute for ground truth--what something actually feels like when you're there.

When you couple decisions that have different timing needs, different information requirements, different stakes for different people, something subtly degrades. Not catastrophically. Just enough that your best customers end up with worse options than before.

And staff ends up mediating problems that used to resolve themselves.

Watch for this in your systems. In your processes. Anywhere you're moving multiple decisions because one system can do both.

The person online can't fall in love with a dropdown list. And by locking the site in, you prevent someone on-site from falling in love with it either. The joy of choosing gets replaced by the transaction of assigning--for everyone.

---

**What would have worked:** Guarantee admission offsite. Keep site selection for arrival. Let people walk around, feel the place, fall in love, and choose.

The system could have done both. It chose not to.

It was an easy choice to make. Harder to notice until you're on the receiving end.
