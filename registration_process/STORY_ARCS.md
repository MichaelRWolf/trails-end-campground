# Story Arc Proposals (Bones Only)

## Terminology Clarification

Three things that got bundled (and can be decoupled):

1. **Registration** = Capacity Commitment + Payment (administrative actions; can move pre-arrival)
2. **Site Selection** = Individual decision (should stay at arrival; needs sensory ground truth)

---

## Arc 1: "The Owner's Vision"

**Hook:**  
The owner built something intentional--a campground with extraordinary variety, each site unique, each suited to different people and moods. The diversity is the point.

**Problem:**  
He identified a real gap: no guaranteed admission scared off potential guests.

**Solution Chosen:**  
Online system for registration (capacity + payment pre-arrival). But it coupled registration with site selection, locking people into choices made without ground truth.

**Stories (pattern):**  
Jan, Steven, Kevin--three arrivals, three people hitting friction because their pre-assigned site doesn't fit reality.

**Insight:**  
Registration and site selection are separate. You can guarantee admission without pre-assigning sites.

**Summary:**  
Good intention. Invisible coupling. The solution improved one thing (peace of mind) and accidentally broke another (autonomy in site selection). A case study in how full-featured systems make it easy to move multiple decisions together without noticing.

---

## Arc 2: "Experience vs. Task"

**Hook:**  
Site selection used to be an experience--you arrived, walked around, felt the vibe, chose collaboratively, set up. It was part of the vacation.

**What Changed:**  
Site selection became a task--a dropdown list decision made at home with a stylized map. You committed before you had ground truth.

**The Loss:**  
Sensory observation, collaboration, autonomy, the joy of finding the perfect fit. Richness → constraint.

**Stories (lens):**  
Jan, Steven, Kevin--all experiencing site selection as a problem to solve rather than an experience to enjoy.

**Insight:**  
Moving a decision early isn't free. It costs you the information and the joy that comes from presence.

**Summary:**  
When you move a decision away from the moment of presence, you lose more than efficiency. You lose the human elements that made it work well. A reminder that not every decision should be moved off-site just because you technically can.

---

## Arc 3: "The Invisible Coupling"

**Hook:**  
It's easy to build a system that does many things. It's hard to notice you're moving multiple decisions when one system moves them all together.

**What Happened:**  
Registration needed to move pre-arrival (pace of mind). The system had features for registration AND site selection. So both moved. No one explicitly said "let's couple these." It just happened.

**The Oversight:**  
Site selection and registration have different optimal timing and information needs. They didn't need to move together. But once the system had the features, they did.

**Stories (illustration):**  
Jan, Steven, Kevin--cases where the coupling caused friction that wouldn't have happened if site selection had stayed at arrival.

**Insight:**  
This is easy to overlook in the middle of a project. You're focused on solving the registration problem. You're not asking "what else just moved?"

**Summary:**  
A design lesson: When you deploy a full-featured system, be explicit about which features you're using and which timing changes you're making. Coupling decisions invisibly is easy. Noticing it is hard. Decoupling later is expensive.

---

## Arc 4: "Freedom and Trust"

**Hook:**  
Systems send signals. A system that says "you need permission from the database to occupy a site" sends a different message than "pick any unoccupied site that works for you."

**What Signals Mean:**  
"Permission from database" = distrust. "Pick what works for you" = trust.

**The Real Cost:**  
Autonomy isn't just functional; it's emotional. People feel the difference between being trusted and being controlled.

**Stories (outcome lens):**  
Jan, Steven, Kevin--all experienced a system that required staff intervention or app friction for what used to be self-serve autonomy.

**Insight:**  
Moving decisions to pre-arrival created more staff time, not less. Because now conflicts happen regularly (site doesn't match reality). Automation that removes autonomy can paradoxically create more work.

**Summary:**  
The best systems trust people to make good choices based on ground truth. They preserve autonomy not just for efficiency but for dignity. This campground's original system did that. The redesign tried to solve one problem and accidentally removed it.

---

## Arc 5: "Decisions Are More Than Outcomes" (Meta-framing)

**Hook:**  
When we talk about "decisions," we usually mean the final result. "Jan chose site 7." But that misses something important. The process of deciding--who decides, when, with what information--affects quality and satisfaction as much as the outcome itself.

**The Case Study:**  
Trails End Campground, a small operation that changed its registration process to improve one stakeholder experience (guests wanting certainty) and accidentally degraded another (guests wanting autonomy and joy).

**The Pattern:**  

- Same outcome (Jan ends up in a site)
- Different process (pre-assigned vs. chosen on-site)
- Very different satisfaction (constrained vs. autonomous)

**Stories:**  
Jan, Steven, Kevin--each showing what the difference between "assigned" and "chosen" actually means in practice.

**Insight:**  
Process matters. Timing matters. Information matters. Who decides matters. These aren't separate from the decision; they're part of what makes it a good decision.

**Summary:**  
For anyone redesigning systems--especially when you're moving decisions in time or location--think about process as carefully as outcomes. What are you changing about *how* this gets decided, not just *what* gets decided? Capacity Commitment and Site Selection look similar (both about "where you'll sleep") but the right timing for each is very different.
